She is the co-author, with Erik M. Conway, of the best-selling books ‘Merchants of Doubt’, and ‘The Big Myth: How American Business Taught Us to Loath Government and Love the Free Market’. &nbsp;‘Merchants of Doubt’ identified parallels between the climate change debate and earlier different public controversies, notably the tobacco industry’s lobbying and propaganda campaign to obscure the link between smoking and lung cancer.</p>

          <div class="views-row"><div class="views-field views-field-title"><h2 class="field-content">How much “significant harm” can a €2 trillion EU budget do?</h2></div><div class="views-field views-field-created"><span class="field-content"><time class="datetime" datetime="2026-08-25T13:44:00+02:00" title="Tuesday, August 25, 2026 - 13:44">25 Aug 2026</time>
</span></div><div class="views-field views-field-field-paragraphs"><div class="field-content">  <div class="paragraph paragraph--type--text paragraph--view-mode--default">
          
            <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-text field--type-text-long field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Sector:</strong> Climate, Finance, Industrial Policy</p>
<p><strong>In a nutshell:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Next EU budget contains dangerous exemptions to the 'Do No Significant Harm' principle.</li>
<li>Mining industry and fossil fuel lobby pushed for loopholes, and are set to benefit with public money for harmful projects and false solutions that promote fossil fuels.&nbsp;</li>
</ul>
<dl class="ckeditor-accordion">
<dt><strong>What you need to know</strong></dt>
<dd>
<p>In July, the European Commission <a href="https://commission.europa.eu/strategy-and-policy/eu-budget/long-term-eu-budget/eu-budget-2028-2034_en">presented its proposal</a> for the EU’s 2028-2034 budget, the next Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF). Amounting to nearly €2 trillion, it features an alarming concession to some of Europe’s most polluting and dangerous industries. Namely, a series of exceptions to the ‘D<em>o No Significant Harm</em>’ (DNSH) principle, which is <a href="https://interreg.eu/news-stories/commission-launches-targeted-consultation-on-do-no-significant-harm-principle-for-2028-2034-mff/">meant to ensure</a> that EU funding does not support activities that significantly harm environmental and climate objectives.</p>
<p>DNSH was incorporated into the financial rules for the EU budget in 2024, in accordance with the EU’s 2020 sustainable finance taxonomy. The <a href="https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32020R0852">EU Taxonomy</a> requires that for economic activities to be considered sustainable, they must not significantly harm any of <a href="https://finance.ec.europa.eu/sustainable-finance/tools-and-standards/eu-taxonomy-sustainable-activities_en">six environmental objectives</a>, including climate change mitigation, pollution prevention and biodiversity protection.&nbsp;</p>
<p>The corporate lobby world, however, was never keen on not doing significant harm: big business groups <a href="https://ert.eu/documents/simplifying-the-eu-taxonomy-ert-response/">pushed to simplify DNSH criteria</a>, while oil and gas lobbies <a href="https://iogpeurope.org/resource/iogp-europe-response-to-the-eu-taxonomy-review-of-climate-and-environmental-delegated-acts-consultation/">complained</a> of “disproportionate administrative burdens”. Enter the Commission’s new deregulatory tool, <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/04/what-corporate-capture-looks">the omnibus</a>, deregulating handfuls of laws in one go, guided by industry. The very first omnibus targeted corporate sustainability rules including the EU Taxonomy, with promises to “simplify” the application of the DNSH principle it introduced.&nbsp;</p>
<p>In that context, it is perhaps not surprising that the <a href="https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/2509/oj/eng">new rules</a> for the EU budget require that EU funded programmes and activities only follow the DNSH principle “where feasible and appropriate”. Recent <a href="https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/ec0e871c-9bfa-43de-9f96-517614616487_en?filename=Concept%20paper%20public%20consultation%20on%20Do%20No%20Significant%20Harm.pdf">draft guidelines</a> – appearing after a <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/info/law/better-regulation/have-your-say/initiatives/17112-Guidance-on-applying-the-do-no-significant-harm-principle-under-the-2028-2034-multiannual-financial-framework/feedback_en_en?p_id=22453">public consultation</a> earlier this year, and now the subject of a <a href="https://interreg.eu/news-stories/commission-launches-targeted-consultation-on-do-no-significant-harm-principle-for-2028-2034-mff/">consultation</a> of the EU’s <a href="https://interreg.eu/about/types-of-programmes/">funding programmes</a> – set out what this could mean.</p>
<p>And the prognosis is not good: colossal loopholes to the DNSH principle allow EU taxpayers’ money to be used in a way that causes significant harm to the climate, water resources, biodiversity and ecosystems. They suggest that applying DNSH would not be “feasible or appropriate” in crisis situations, for defence and security, and for other reasons of “overriding public interest”. Certainly, this not the first place that military exemptions to environmental protection have been introduced (take, for example, <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/C:/Users/alway/AppData/C:/Users/alway/Dropbox/CEO%202025-26/Deregulation-Death%20star%20general%20stuff/Deregulation%20Watch/Defence%20as%20a%20justification%20for%20deregulation:%20the%20military%20caveat%20in%20the%20Climate%20Law">the Climate Law</a>). And CEO has <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/05/permission-pollute">documented the expanding application of so-called ‘public interest’</a> to more and more problematic and polluting industries, at the request of those very same industries. Even <a href="https://www.politico.eu/article/italy-push-weaken-green-rules-eu-budget/">Politico has noted</a> that the Commission’s plans to exempt projects judged to be “overriding public interest” from DNSH could open the door for data centers or critical raw materials to benefit from favorable treatment.</p>
<p>Music to the ears of mining lobby group Euromines – an influential player in the <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/05/permission-pollute">industry push to weaken rules</a> protecting community rights and the environment so that it becomes much easier to get permits. Euromines describes how it <a href="https://euromines.org/category/news/">actively pushed</a> against the use of the DNSH principle “as a negative filter for accessing EU funding”, an “exclusionary tool” that could limit mining corporations’ access to EU public money.</p>
<p>Another happy customer will be the fossil fuel lobby – due to the many additional specific exceptions provided in the draft guidelines. ‘Carve-outs’ are provided for a list of activities that do significant harm, such as fossil fuel production, transport and burning for electricity. These exceptions, however, would allow funding of fossil fuel-enabling infrastructure via the <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/carboncoup">false promise of carbon capture technology</a>. For example, there are carve-outs for ‘low-carbon’ energy and hydrogen infrastructure, by which they mean fossil gas or fossil-based hydrogen in combination with carbon capture and storage, a <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/05/permission-pollute">risky, costly and repeatedly failed</a> technology. This echoes demands by fossil fuel lobbyists such as <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/info/law/better-regulation/have-your-say/initiatives/17112-Guidance-on-applying-the-do-no-significant-harm-principle-under-the-2028-2034-multiannual-financial-framework/F33386979_en">Gas Distributors for Sustainability</a>, <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/info/law/better-regulation/have-your-say/initiatives/17112-Guidance-on-applying-the-do-no-significant-harm-principle-under-the-2028-2034-multiannual-financial-framework/F33383774_en">Italgas</a> and <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/info/law/better-regulation/have-your-say/initiatives/17112-Guidance-on-applying-the-do-no-significant-harm-principle-under-the-2028-2034-multiannual-financial-framework/F33387460_en">Snam</a>, as well as <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/info/law/better-regulation/have-your-say/initiatives/17112-Guidance-on-applying-the-do-no-significant-harm-principle-under-the-2028-2034-multiannual-financial-framework/F33387073_en">HydrogenEurope</a>. Likewise, “net-zero technologies” – which <a href="https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ:L_202401735">explicitly include</a> hydrogen and carbon capture – for industrial processes and fossil power generation get a free pass, as do waste incinerators using carbon capture and storage.</p>
<p>The next EU budget is going to be a hot topic for the coming year, and these steps to remove vital safeguards against harm will need continued scrutiny.</p>
</dd>
</dl>

    <div class="views-row"><div class="views-field views-field-title"><h2 class="field-content">Implementation dialogue on carbon capture: latest in a textbook case of fossil fuel influence</h2></div><div class="views-field views-field-created"><span class="field-content"><time class="datetime" datetime="2026-07-22T10:36:00+02:00" title="Wednesday, July 22, 2026 - 10:36">22 Jul 2026</time>
</span></div><div class="views-field views-field-field-paragraphs"><div class="field-content">  <div class="paragraph paragraph--type--text paragraph--view-mode--default">
          
            <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-text field--type-text-long field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Sector:</strong> Energy/climate</p>
<p><strong>In a nutshell:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Climate Commissioner Hoekstra organised an ‘implementation dialogue’ on carbon capture and storage in EU climate policy, inviting mostly representatives of dirty industries with vested interests.</li>
<li>When asked to bring in critical voices, Hoekstra's Cabinet falsely claimed that they had invited a diversity of stakeholders.</li>
<li>This is one more step in a long list of biased decision-making promoting a failed, risky and staggeringly expensive technology.</li>
</ul>
</div>

,   <div class="paragraph paragraph--type--image paragraph--view-mode--default">

,   <div class="paragraph paragraph--type--text paragraph--view-mode--default">
          
            <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-text field--type-text-long field--label-hidden field__item"><dl class="ckeditor-accordion">
<dt><strong>What you need to know</strong>&nbsp;</dt>
<dd>
<p>Business-dominated <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/REPORT_CORPORATE%20CAPTURE.pdf">implementation dialogues</a> are a staple of the Commission’s deregulation wave, and on 29 June, Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra held one on <a href="https://climate.ec.europa.eu/citizens-stakeholders/events/implementation-dialogue-carbon-capture-and-storage-eu-climate-policy-barriers-and-enablers-2026-06-29_en">Carbon Capture and Storage in EU Climate Policy - Barriers and Enablers</a>.&nbsp;</p>
<p>For the past three years, the EU has been turbo-charging policies to support the massive scale-up of Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) technologies – via public money, permitting deregulation, and dramatically upping carbon capture targets. These targets stand in stark contrast to reality: globally, we’re “permanently” burying <a href="https://projects.propublica.org/why-carbon-capture-cant-solve-climate-change/">less CO2</a> than a single large power plant can emit in a year. And there’s no reason to believe this is going to change – CCS technologies have been trialled for over 50 years with negligible results.</p>
<p>Corporate influence is a major reason that the Commission is betting on carbon capture despite ample evidence against it: fossil fuel and energy-intensive industries have fed a mirage that it will help achieve EU climate targets and decarbonise their continued use of fossil fuels.</p>
<p>This flawed premise was the basis of Hoekstra's implementation dialogue, which identified challenges slowing down CCS deployment as “lack of funding, slow permitting and negative public perception.” The dialogue claimed to have wide stakeholder participation, but when we wrote to Hoekstra's cabinet asking to participate, they declined. They argued that “the organisations invited were chosen to respect a diversity in terms of stakeholders, size, and geographical balance. In order to ensure a meaningful conversation, we had to limit the dialogue to a fixed number of participants. We regret that this time your organisation was not selected.”</p>
<p>At the time, the list of participants was not available, though it was published after our request. Contrary to the diversity claimed by the Commission, the dialogue was hugely dominated by fossil fuel and energy intensive industries, with only one trade union, few regional authorities and just two NGOs, both very supportive of CCS (<a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/carboncoup">and with a history of</a> links to the fossil fuel industry).</p>
<p>Fossil fuel participants included Fluxys, Gasunie, Equinor, Eni and INEOS, as well as oil and gas lobby IOGP, and CCS Europe. Energy-intensive industries were also present, such as cement lobby Cembureau and its member Heidelberg, steel firm Marcegaglia, and industrial gas company <a href="https://it.airliquide.com/noi/news/air-liquide-accoglie-con-favore-il-riconoscimento-di-callisto-come-progetto-europeo-di-interesse-comune">AirLiquide</a>. The latter three – along with Italian oil giant <a href="https://www.eni.com/en-IT/media/press-release/2023/11/eni-ravenna-ccs-project.html">Eni</a> – all have vested interests in Italy’s <a href="https://www.recommon.org/en/ravennas-false-solution/">Ravenna CCS/Callisto</a> project. The ten Omnibus packages launched in 2025 were prepared without an impact assessment, instead using 'targeted consultation' with handpicked corporate lobbyists rather than the inclusive public consultation methods outlined in the Better Regulation Guidelines. In a detailed assessment, Professor <a href="https://verfassungsblog.de/deregulating-better-regulation/">Alberto Alemanno</a> warns of “constitutional regression” and questions “whether this new policy is compatible with Article 11(3) of the EU Treaty”, which requires “broad consultations with parties concerned”. The Commission’s use of ‘targeted consultations’, Alemanno warns, “systematically favours well-resourced stakeholders.” For details on the Commission’s use of ‘targeted consultations’ to produce deregulation proposals at high speed, see <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/REPORT_CORPORATE%20CAPTURE.pdf">CEO’s indepth report published last month</a>.</p>

<dd>
<p>After a marathon negotiation that lasted until the early morning of 7 May, the EU Parliament and national governments reached a deal to water down the Artificial Intelligence Act. As Corporate Europe Observatory and LobbyControl previously <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/01/article-article-how-big-tech-shaped-eus-roll-back-digital-rights">documented</a>, the Commission’s Digital Omnibus proposal closely mirrored key lobby demands from Big Tech companies. In recent weeks, severe tensions between member states and a breakdown in negotiations were caused by a lobbying push from European industry, including Siemens and Bosch, headed by the tech lobby group DigitalEurope, to exempt a range of AI applications.<br /><br />The deal marks a severe weakening of key protections and adds legal complexity, but will likely benefit large industrial players, and Big Tech companies.</p>
<p><strong>Adding legal complexity</strong></p>
<p>In March, the European Parliament added a highly contentious amendment to the AI omnibus that would exempt products covered by other sectoral legislation, including machinery, toys, and medical devices, from the AI Act. Stunningly, AI requirements would then be re-introduced at some future unspecified date via twelve separate sectoral regulatory regimes, greatly increasing legal uncertainty and complexity, but, of course, creating large loopholes for big business.</p>
<p>In an open letter co-signed by the CEO, a group of 36 civil society organisations warned that the Parliament’s proposal would not only weaken product safety and consumer protection, but also “introduce unnecessary complexity and uncertainty into the EU governance of AI”. While most national governments resisted the change, German Chancellor Merz intervened directly in the negotiations, and <a href="https://www.heise.de/en/news/Chancellor-Merz-Make-European-AI-regulation-easier-11264952.html">together</a> with industrial giants such as Siemens, ASML and Bosch, exerted exceptional pressure to get the amendment approved. After a week-long standoff, machinery was exempted from the AI Act as part of a final compromise, but not other products such as medical devices.</p>
<p>In a reaction, the digital rights organisation EDRi hit the nail on the head: “As with other Omnibus proposals, the message is clear: when powerful actors complain loudly enough, safeguards can be recast as burdens, and rules that protect people can be reopened.”</p>
<p><strong>Undermining fundamental rights</strong></p>
<p>The AI Omnibus will also postpone the implementation of high-risk AI obligations until December 2027 – a key Big Tech <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/01/article-article-how-big-tech-shaped-eus-roll-back-digital-rights">demand</a>. This will allow companies to continue releasing risky AI systems onto the market without any safeguards. The risks are far from hypothetical, ranging from <a href="https://www.bbc.com/news/business-54698858">algorithmic-powered employee firings</a> to <a href="https://www.axios.com/2020/08/19/england-exams-algorithm-grading">biased algorithms that disadvantage students</a> based on their socio-economic background. Highly problematic AI systems are already in circulation.&nbsp;</p>
<p>Fortunately, the European Parliament and member states rejected some egregious proposals. For example, a Commission proposal to remove the public database in which tech companies must register the AI systems these companies deem exempt from high-risk classification was rejected. The <a href="https://www.edf-feph.org/publications/a-call-to-eu-legislators-protect-rights-and-reject-the-call-to-delete-transparency-safeguard-in-ai-act/">estimated cost savings of €100 per company</a> were so low that only the EU Commission thought this was a good idea.</p>
<p>The Grok deepfake scandal also created the necessary backlash to garner support for a ban on nudifier apps, even though questions remain about the <a href="https://www.techpolicy.press/eu-moves-to-regulate-ai-nudification-but-key-challenges-remain/">effectiveness</a> of the proposed ban.</p>
<p>The deregulation of the AI Act is only the first step in the Commission's far-reaching and misguided plans to weaken the EU’s digital rulebook in an attempt to please big business and the MAGA controlled White House.</p>
</dd>
</dl>

    <div class="views-row"><div class="views-field views-field-title"><h2 class="field-content">The EU’s 'One Europe, One Market Roadmap': a gargantuan deregulation push that’s leading Europe down a dead end</h2></div><div class="views-field views-field-created"><span class="field-content"><time class="datetime" datetime="2026-04-27T18:15:00+02:00" title="Monday, April 27, 2026 - 18:15">27 Apr 2026</time>
</span></div><div class="views-field views-field-field-paragraphs"><div class="field-content">  <div class="paragraph paragraph--type--text paragraph--view-mode--default">
          
            <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-text field--type-text-long field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>In a nutshell:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>&nbsp;the European Parliament and governments secured significant last minute changes to the Commission’s draft 'One Europe, One Market Roadmap', signed during last week's EU summit in Cyprus</li>
<li>quite a few terrible deregulation commitments that were in the draft roadmap were removed during last-minute talks</li>
<li>but the Commission got green light for Single Market deregulation on ten areas – and by signing the roadmap, parliament and governments have agreed to rush Omnibuses, "EU Inc" and a huge number of other deregulation initiatives through EU decision-making at unprecedented speed</li>
</ul>
<dl class="ckeditor-accordion">
<dt><strong>What you need to know</strong></dt>
<dd>
<p>In the run-up to the Cyprus summit the EU, <a href="https://www.euractiv.com/news/exclusive-eu-rolls-back-ambition-in-new-single-market-plan/">as Euractiv put it</a>, "radically scaled down the ambition" of <a href="https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/5445de81-9481-4335-9902-9756159ba614_en?filename=one-europe-one-market-roadmap.pdf">the “One Europe, One Market” roadmap</a>. The Commission’s proposal to include binding "targets for final adoption" of deregulation initiatives was "downgraded to target dates for reaching a political agreement". Due to <a href="https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/ukraine-upstages-middle-east-at-eu-summit/">pressure from progressive MEPs</a> references to the “European Pillar of social rights” and a future review “to encompass the social dimension of the Single Market” were added to the roadmap. Some deeply problematic proposals originating from the list of demands presented by hard-right government leaders Merz and Meloni earlier this year (see Deregulation Watch <strong>March 13<sup>th</sup> 2026) </strong>were removed from the roadmap.</p>
<p>In the draft roadmap, the three EU institutions were to “commit to a structural reduction of administrative burdens” as well as seven specific commitments, such as ”refraining from introducing new obstacles or barriers, including through goldplating and unduly divergent national approaches”. A blanket promise to refrain from 'new obstacles or barriers' would have been hugely problematic, because what constitutes an ‘obstacle’ or ‘barrier’ in the Single Market is often a matter of political interpretation. <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/sites/default/files/2023-06/30%20Years%20of%20EU%20Single%20Market-Report-Final.pdf">Corporate lobby groups routinely file complaints</a> against entirely legitimate national government rules on social or environmental protection, arguing these are Single Market 'barriers' that should be removed, expecting the European Commission to intervene. One example is the Commission’s intervention against the French government’s new law to tackle the growing problem of hugely wasteful and polluting 'fast fashion'. Following complaints by corporate lobby groups, the Commission paused the final adoption of the French law until changes are made. In <a href="https://eeb.org/en/eu-commission-must-not-curtail-member-states-from-taking-on-fashion-overproduction/">a letter last month</a>, 65 civil society groups defended the French law as “a unique opportunity to truly tackle the harmful model of overproduction in the clothing industry”.”</p>
<p>The proposed ban on so-called ‘goldplating’ was an attempt to close the space available to member states to introduce more ambitious social and environmental standards than those agreed in EU level directives (see also Deregulation Watch March 13th 2026). Luckily this was removed from the roadmap, although the Commission and hard-right governments are likely to try to get this back on the agenda before long.</p>
<p><a href="https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/5445de81-9481-4335-9902-9756159ba614_en?filename=one-europe-one-market-roadmap.pdf">The roadmap</a> still contains a huge number of problematic deregulatory initiatives and therefore requires critical scrutiny and public debate. There’s an appendix of over 40 'priority deliverables', competitiveness-related initiatives that the Commission has already launched (such as the ten Omnibus packages introduced in 2025 and the proposal for a new 'EU Inc.' company law status) or planned initiatives such as new Omnibus packages on energy and taxation. Worryingly, the list also includes a new banking deregulation package to be adopted before the end of 2027. By signing the roadmap, Parliament and governments “commit to working towards a swift agreement on all the legislative proposals set out in the annex, treating them as political priorities”.</p>
<p>The roadmap includes a commitment to “a more integrated Single Market including by removing the ten most harmful barriers’. This refers to what the Commission has termed <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/attachment/881209/Factsheet%20-%20Single%20Market%20Strategy.pdf">'the terrible 10 barriers'</a>. These are not specific barriers but ten broad problem areas, including 'restrictive and diverging national services regulation', with a focus on specific service sectors such as construction and retail. It’s worth remembering that a <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/2020/11/wakeup-call-european-commission-its-failed-power-grab-over-local-services">previous Commission initiative to deregulate national-level service rules</a> was defeated in 2020. Opponents of the Services Notification Directive included the mayors of Barcelona, Amsterdam and Paris, who were rightly concerned that the directive would restrict their ability to regulate AirBnB's activities, which were causing significant housing affordability issues in their cities. Worryingly, <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/attachment/881209/Factsheet%20-%20Single%20Market%20Strategy.pdf">the Commission’s document</a> on dismantling the 'terrible ten barriers' makes no mention of preserving social and environmental protection, or the right of public authorities to regulate in the public interest.</p>
</dd>
</dl>
</div>
      
      </div>

    <div class="views-row"><div class="views-field views-field-title"><h2 class="field-content">Food and environmental safety dillution through European Biotech Act?</h2></div><div class="views-field views-field-created"><span class="field-content"><time class="datetime" datetime="2026-04-24T18:14:00+02:00" title="Friday, April 24, 2026 - 18:14">24 Apr 2026</time>
</span></div><div class="views-field views-field-field-paragraphs"><div class="field-content">  <div class="paragraph paragraph--type--text paragraph--view-mode--default">
          
            <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-text field--type-text-long field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>In a nutshell:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>The Biotech Act I starts to gnaw away at past achievements to stop industry from hiding scientific data showing harm of their products (chemicals, GMOs, food additives etc.)</li>
<li>It means a further roll back of the EU GMO laws - in particular for GM micro-organisms&nbsp;</li>
</ul>
<dl class="ckeditor-accordion">
<dt><strong>What you need to know</strong></dt>
<dd>
<p>The <a href="https://health.ec.europa.eu/publications/proposal-regulation-establish-measures-strengthen-unions-biotechnology-and-biomanufacturing-sectors_en">Biotech Act I proposal</a> was announced by EC-president Ursula von der Leyen in the <a href="https://commission.europa.eu/priorities-2024-2029_en">political priorities for 2024-2029</a> and was published on 16 December 2025. While its main focus is on health biotechnology, its impact is much broader. The Act proposes changes to six existing safety regulations. Notably it takes aim at some important improvements to the General Food Law (GFL) following the successful 2019 ‘Stop Glyphosate European Citizens Initiative’ (ECI).&nbsp;</p>
<p>Then-Health Commissioner Vytenis Andriukaitis <a href="https://citizens-initiative-forum.europa.eu/citizens-experiences/blogs/how-stop-glyphosate-brought-about-small-revolution_en">introduced measures</a> to force companies to publish all scientific data contained in the studies for market approval. In addition, all regulatory studies submitted by industry had to be notified, blocking companies’ ability to abort and hide studies that show harmful effects by simply leaving them out of their application dossiers. With the proposed changes (see <a href="https://health.ec.europa.eu/publications/proposal-regulation-establish-measures-strengthen-unions-biotechnology-and-biomanufacturing-sectors_en">here</a>, p.27) the punishment for failing to notify a study is reduced from a six to a three-month delay in authorization. A three-month delay is hardly a punishment for a company, if it grants the opportunity to leave out inconvenient studies.&nbsp;</p>
<p>In addition, a company can ask EFSA for advice on study design, making the agency co-responsible, while previously this responsibility remained with the company. And while previously EFSA staff giving advice to applicants could not be involved in the actual risk assessment to avoid conflicts of interest, this has been taken out in the Biotech Act.<br /><br /><strong>Safety presumed, not proven</strong></p>
<p>The Biotech Act is also the next step in a roll back of the existing EU GMO law, following the deregulation for NGT plants, a proposal that is currently in the <a href="https://friendsoftheearth.eu/press-release/eu-vote-on-new-gmos-wrong-for-farmers-wrong-for-consumers-wrong-for-nature/">final phase</a> of decision making following a <a href="https://corporateeurope.org/en/2021/03/derailing-eu-rules-new-gmos">strong industry lobby push</a>.&nbsp;<br /><br />With this Act the Commission proposes to <a href="https://www.testbiotech.org/en/news/european-commission-plans-to-accelerate-the-release-of-genetically-engineered-microorganisms/">lower environmental safety standards</a> for the release of genetically engineered micro-organisms (GMMs) into the environment. The market approval would be time-unlimited (renewal assessments are scrapped). Detection and identification requirements can be lowered, which would make environmental monitoring more difficult. Safety requirements would be further lowered for a new category of “low-risk GMMs”. GMO veterinary products are even completely removed from EU GMO law.</p>
<p><a name="_GoBack"></a> <a href="https://www.testbiotech.org/en/news/european-commission-plans-to-accelerate-the-release-of-genetically-engineered-microorganisms/">Testbiotech argues</a> that the use of new genetic engineering techniques (NGTs) and of AI combined have substantially expanded the ways in which microorganisms can be engineered, and “therefore the safety standards should be raised, not lowered”.&nbsp;</p>
<p>No impact assessment has been published. There is mention of 25 stakeholder interviews, without any further information. A Biotech Act II is scheduled for later this year and is expected to include more deregulation measures for biotech products developed for other sectors like agriculture, pushed for by <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/posts/croplife-europe_joint-industry-statement-on-the-eu-biotech-activity-7445378072830296065-UO5b/">the biotech lobby group Croplife Europe</a>.&nbsp;</p>
</dd>
</dl>
</div>
      
      </div>

    <div class="views-row"><div class="views-field views-field-title"><h2 class="field-content">The dreaded blueprint for social dumping</h2></div><div class="views-field views-field-created"><span class="field-content"><time class="datetime" datetime="2026-03-24T11:36:00+01:00" title="Tuesday, March 24, 2026 - 11:36">24 Mar 2026</time>
</span></div><div class="views-field views-field-field-paragraphs"><div class="field-content">  <div class="paragraph paragraph--type--text paragraph--view-mode--default">
          
            <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-text field--type-text-long field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Sector:</strong> general, company law</p>
<p><strong>In a nutshell:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>The Commission has presented its proposal for a '28th regime', that will introduce a European company form with its own rulebook.</li>
<li>The EU Inc. proposal will enable companies to ignore labour rights.</li>
</ul>
<dl class="ckeditor-accordion">
<dt><strong>What you need to know</strong></dt>
<dd>
<p>The Commission’s proposal on the 28<sup>th</sup> regime has been released, and the initial signs are worrying.</p>
<p>On Wednesday 18 March the Commission tabled a proposal that had been anticipated with both joy and dread since it was first announced in Ursula von der Leyen’s political guidelines for this term- “a so-called 28th regime to allow companies to benefit from a simpler, harmonised set of rules in certain areas,” <a href="https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/e6cd4328-673c-4e7a-8683-f63ffb2cf648_en?filename=Political%20Guidelines%202024-2029_EN.pdf">the guidelines</a> read.&nbsp;</p>
<p>Technically, it is about introducing a European company form, with easier and faster registration. However, this could provide an easy way of sidestepping rules at the national level, introduced to uphold workers rights. If the national approach be cumbersome, companies can simply choose a different rulebook – a European one, the 28<sup>th</sup> regime.&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Attack on labour rights</strong></p>
<p>The jury is now out on the exact effect of the model picked by the Commission, but analysts in all quarters have taken an initial look at the text to figure out its nature. So far, trade union analysts are not in doubt: labour rights are under attack. &nbsp;</p>
<p>Nevertheless, according to trade unions, there are good reasons to be worried already. Services workers federation UNI Europa regional secretary Oliver Roetig said in <a href="https://www.uni-europa.org/news/eu-inc-a-free-pass-for-regime-shopping/">a statement</a>, that with “companies allowed to cherry-pick countries with lower standards, it risks undermining our European social model, industrial relations and quality jobs. The proposal is unfortunately very clear: employee participation rights are linked to where the company is registered, and the company can register where they want.”</p>
<p>General Secretary of the European Federation of Building and Wood Workers Tom Deleu <a href="https://www.efbww.eu/publications/press-releases/letterbox-inc-in-the-making-commission-proposal-ignores-fraud-re/4961-a">agrees</a>: “This is a bad proposal. In sectors like construction, EU Inc. will quickly turn into ‘Letterbox Inc’: it will fuel social dumping, weaken enforcement, and put workers at risk,” he said.&nbsp;</p>
<p>The European Trade Union Confederation (ETUC) has <a href="https://www.eunews.it/en/2026/03/18/businesses-are-pleased-trade-unions-are-concerned-politicians-are-divided-reactions-to-the-eus-28th-regime/">highlighted</a> the absence of provisions that could have prevented companies from circumventing national labour laws, thereby endangering collective bargaining, wages, and rights to information and consultation in peril.&nbsp;</p>
<p><strong>Triumphant business groups</strong></p>
<p>For the business community, the Commission comes bearing gifts. There is already a hint in the name of the proposal, <a href="https://www.eu-inc.org/">EU Inc.</a>, taken straight from the lobby group set up by finance and industry companies to promote the idea. BusinessEurope, the main employers’ association, is satisfied, not least because what was first presented by the Commission President as a measure to support “innovative companies” ended up as something much broader. BusinessEurope’s Secretary General Markus Beyrer <a href="https://www.businesseurope.eu/publications/28th-regime-proposal-a-positive-development-for-competitiveness-across-the-single-market/">notes with satisfaction</a> that the proposed new rulebook is “open to all types of companies.”</p>
<p><strong>Similar plans have been defeated</strong></p>
<p>It is not the first time the trade union movement faces a challenge like this, and previous attempts have been defeated. That could happen again – through campaigns waged by labour or more cross-cutting efforts. It does not take opposition from so many governments to defeat it.&nbsp;</p>
</dd>
</dl>
